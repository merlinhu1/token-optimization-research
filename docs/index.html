Independent test · published 11 October 2026

Fifteen products promise to cut your AI coding bill. Seven of them worked.

And even that comes with a catch. Six of the seven only proved themselves with one AI assistant on one codebase. The honest headline is not that these tools fail — it is that the savings they advertise are usually smaller than the random variation you get from running the same job twice.

What we found How we tested Full technical report

If you have never heard of any of this

AI coding assistants are programs that write and fix software for you. To change one line in a real project, an assistant may read dozens of files first — and you are billed for every chunk of text it reads and writes. Those chunks are called tokens. On a serious task the bill adds up quickly.

So a small industry has appeared selling add-ons that promise to cut that bill: they compress what the assistant reads, index your code so it searches instead of browsing, or trim the conversation as it grows. Most of the savings figures you see for these products come from the companies selling them.

We are not one of those companies. We do not sell any of these products and none of them paid for or saw this test. We ran the same programming jobs over and over — once with each add-on installed and once with nothing installed — and compared the bills.

What we found

Three things, in order of how confident we are about them.

1. One product worked everywhere we looked

LeanCTX cut the bill under all three assistants we tested — by 25%, 35% and 65% — and it is the only product that managed that. But all three of those results come from the same codebase, so "everywhere" means three assistants, not three kinds of project.

2. Six more worked in one specific setup

Token Savior, Caveman, Serena, Cartog, Ponytail and RTK each cut the bill by 14–26%, but only with Claude Code on that same codebase. We cannot say whether they work anywhere else, because where we checked, the result did not hold up.

3. For the other eight, we cannot tell you

CodeGraph, CodeScope, Graphify, RepoWise, SigMap, Snip, TokenJuice and jCodeMunch produced no result we could confirm. Several of their readings looked like large savings. None of them survived a closer look.

That means unproven, not useless. It is a real and important difference.

The single most useful thing to take away: which AI assistant you use affects the bill at least as much as which add-on you install. The same product cut costs by a quarter under one assistant and made them worse under another. There is no product here we can recommend without also naming the assistant you are running it with.

There are two different ways to save, and most tools only do one

An assistant's bill is simply how many steps it takes multiplied by how much each step costs. Splitting the saving into those two halves tells you what a product is actually doing — and they turn out to work in genuinely different ways.

Fewer steps

Caveman and Ponytail save by getting the assistant to the answer in less back-and-forth — Caveman cuts 69 steps to 52. Each step still costs what it always did.

Cheaper steps

Token Savior, Serena, Cartog and RTK do the opposite: the assistant works through roughly the same number of steps, but each one is cheaper because it is handling less text.

Both at once — rare

Only LeanCTX under Claude Code did both. Under the other two assistants it took more steps and still won overall, because it made each step so much cheaper — on OpenCode, 8,257 down to 2,792.

This matters if you are choosing one. A "fewer steps" tool is doing something to how the assistant works; a "cheaper steps" tool is doing something to what it reads. The two are not interchangeable, and a product that helps on one codebase may have nothing to trim on yours.

Why we could only confirm seven out of fifteen

Not because the other eight failed. Because of a problem that affects every number on this page, including the good ones.

Run the exact same programming job twice, with no add-on installed at all, and you do not get the same bill. You get two different bills. We ran those no-add-on comparison jobs repeatedly to find out how different.

The answer was uncomfortable. In the best case the two bills were 9% apart. In the worst case — Claude Code on the Beets codebase — they were 54% apart. Nothing changed between those runs except the assistant's own choices.

So here is the bar a product has to clear. If a tool claims to save 20%, and the same job varies by 54% on its own, a single run showing "20% cheaper" tells you almost nothing. It could be the tool. It could just as easily be the same randomness you see with nothing installed. We only count a result when we ran it at least twice and the saving was bigger than that background variation.

We also tested whether that bar was too strict, by taking the five most borderline single-run results — the ones that only just looked like savings — and running each a second time. Four of the five disappeared. One product that looked 26% cheaper came back at 11%. Another that looked 17% cheaper came back at 1%. The one result that did survive had the narrowest margin of the five, and the widest margin was among those that failed — so a big-looking number on a single run told us nothing about whether it was real.

There is one more thing we would rather not report, because it makes our own numbers less comfortable: every single time we added another no-add-on run to check this background variation, the variation got wider, never narrower. On one setup it went from 17% to 24%; on another from 2% to 15%, which wiped out six results we had previously counted as real. We have no reason to think we have seen the full range yet.

Two patterns are worth pointing out. First, look along any row: the same product routinely helps under one assistant and hurts under another. Second, look at the Beets columns: not one result on that codebase could be confirmed, under any assistant. Whatever these products do, we could not show them doing it on that project.

Coverage is uneven and we have not smoothed it over. Some products have more runs behind them than others — and not at random: when an early run looked promising, we spent budget repeating it, and when it looked bad, we usually did not. So a product with more runs behind it is not a better product, it is one we chose to look at harder. Cells marked not tested are combinations we could not run at all: the product has no working integration for that assistant, so there was nothing to measure.

What this test does not tell you

Written out plainly, because every one of these would be easy to overclaim.

  • It is not a ranking. Six of the seven confirmed products land between 14% and 26% cheaper, all measured against the same comparison. That 11-point range is narrower than the 14% run-to-run variation of the comparison itself, so the apparent ordering between them is not something this data supports.
  • "Not confirmed" does not mean "does not work." Eight products have no confirmed result. That is a statement about how much evidence we gathered, not a verdict on the product. Most of them simply have one run where two or more were needed.
  • Almost everything rests on one codebase. Every confirmed result is from Fastify. Nothing was confirmed on Beets. A product tested on one project has not been tested on yours.
  • We cannot tell you why any of this happens. We measured what the bill did, not what caused it. On OpenCode, several products changed the bill substantially while the assistant never visibly called them once — so whatever is happening is often invisible even to us.
  • Two of the three assistants run the same underlying AI model. Codex CLI and OpenCode both use GPT-5.6-sol; Claude Code uses Claude Opus 5. So when Claude Code behaves differently, we cannot separate the assistant from the model inside it.
  • Correct work was not a condition for counting the bill. 861 of 894 individual tasks were completed correctly. We counted the cost either way, because excluding the expensive failures would flatter every product. Nearly all failures were on Beets, and twenty were a single task that Claude Code fails with no add-on installed at all.
  • Prices change, products change. This is a snapshot taken in 2026. The assistants were frozen at fixed versions for the whole test — one of them auto-updated partway through an earlier round and we had to discard that work.

How we tested

The same recipe every time, so that the only thing that changes between two runs is the add-on being tested.

  1. Pick real work. We took two real open-source projects and broke six genuine behaviours in each — bugs drawn from the projects' own histories, each one caught by a test the project already had. So there is always an objective answer to "did the assistant fix it?"
  2. Write the instructions once. Each job describes the symptom and the expected behaviour. It never names the file, the function or the test to look at — being told where to look would remove exactly the searching these add-ons are supposed to make cheaper. Every assistant gets byte-for-byte identical instructions, and nothing ever tells it to use the add-on.
  3. Install each product properly. Whatever the makers recommend — their plugin, their instructions, their settings — we install it as documented. We never nudge the assistant to use it. If it goes unused, that is a real outcome and we report it.
  4. Run with, and without. Each product is tested on its own, never stacked with another, against the same assistant running the same jobs with nothing installed.
  5. Repeat, and publish what comes back. We never re-ran a job because we disliked the number it produced. Every run we kept is published, including the bad ones. When a run was ruined by our own equipment — a broken setting, a dead connection, a full disk — we deleted it, said so, and recorded what it cost us.
How we count the bill. Text the assistant has already seen is billed at a fraction of new text, and text it writes costs several times more than text it reads. Adding raw token counts together would therefore be misleading. Every figure on this page is a weighted token cost: new text read + 0.1 × re-read text + 6 × text written. It is a cost model, not a specific vendor's price list, and it is the only measure we use anywhere.
